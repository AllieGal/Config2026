# Designing money: how to build a trustworthy brand in the age of AI

**Speaker:** Chris, Creative Lead, Plaid  
**Source:** `talks/designing-money-how-to-build-a-trustworthy-brand-in-the-age-of-ai/transcript.md`

---

## A) Summary

Chris from Plaid's design team traces how the company built a trustworthy visual brand identity in the age of AI, drawing inspiration from historical currency design — specifically guilloche patterns and woodcut engraving — as a visual language of authenticity. The talk covers Plaid's year-long in-house rebrand, in which AI was used to generate concepts, scale pattern systems, and accelerate creative ideation, while human artists handled nuanced, detail-dependent final work. The central argument is that trust is built not just through what an asset looks like, but how it was made — and that AI accelerates ideas without necessarily compressing the deliberate, slow, intentional work that earns trust. The talk also touches on a broader observation: as AI dominates the digital experience, investment in physical, in-person brand experiences is sharply increasing at Plaid.

---

## B) Key points and tools

- **Guilloche patterns**: intricate, mechanically generated interlacing line patterns found on currency worldwide; historically served as forgery-proof visual algorithms; Plaid adopted and modernized them as a core brand element
- **Woodcut / steel engraving illustration style**: historically used in currency printing; associated with sophistication, legacy, and trust; Plaid engaged a hand illustrator (Tatiana Cherkos) to produce work in this style
- **AI as a concept accelerant**: used to generate initial illustration mock-ups, explore physical space designs, and pitch creative directions quickly; acknowledged as a baseline-setter, not a final-asset producer
- **Vibe coding**: mentioned as part of how Plaid tested, built, and refined their procedurally generated pattern system
- **Meticulous hand-drawn cell animation**: Jess Roden, Plaid's cell animator, hand-draws each animation frame — a process the speaker says AI animation tools cannot currently replicate
- **AI for physical space design**: Plaid uses image generators, cloud code, and Figma to design and render event spaces before physically building them
- **Plaid's approach to artist contracts**: explicitly negotiating AI usage rules with creative partners upfront (can we use AI to learn from your art? do you concept with AI? what is your value system around AI?)
- **Financial fraud stat**: first-party fraud now accounts for ~36% of all reported global financial fraud incidents, up from 15% the prior year — context for a Plaid out-of-home campaign
- **Key stance**: "AI is a paintbrush, not a painting. The tool was never the craft."

## C) Verified takeaways (with quotes)

**1. AI accelerates ideas but not necessarily timelines — it creates room for more intentional, deliberate work.**

This is broadly applicable across roles. For our team, using AI to generate boilerplate, scaffolding, or initial implementations should free up time for deeper thinking on system design and quality — not simply compress delivery timelines.

> "AI accelerates our ideas, but not necessarily our timelines. It just leaves us more room to build with intention and play."

**2. For work where trust is the deliverable, human judgment and craft still determine quality.**

The speaker's distinction between AI as a starting point vs. a finished product is relevant when AI-generated code or architecture touches systems where correctness is non-negotiable (e.g., financial data, inventory integrity, catalog data serving live eCommerce).

> "That final step of hand-drawn detailing really builds trust — not just with our customers, but really with our own design team. The process itself gives us confidence that each piece is made hallucination-free."

**3. Establish AI usage rules with collaborators (and tools) explicitly and upfront.** `[RELEVANCE UNCERTAIN — NEEDS CONFIRMATION]`

Plaid negotiates AI rules with external creative partners before work begins. An analogous pattern for our team could be establishing shared norms around when AI-generated code is acceptable without deeper human review — especially in shared services or contracts that other teams depend on.

> "Some of the things we like to ask them are: can we use AI to learn from your art or not? Can we concept with AI? Do you concept with AI? What is your value system in regards to AI and how can we explicitly agree on the rules of the road ahead of time?"
